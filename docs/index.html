How this is counted

The human cost

  • Patients: the number of people in the US who become eligible for the drug each year, times the wait beyond the benchmark in years.
  • Years of life: those patients, times the extra time patients lived in the drug's randomised trial (median survival with the drug minus median survival without it).
  • Every eligible patient is counted. Not all of them would have taken the drug, and the median gain stands in for the average. Each drug's row shows its numbers and where they come from.
  • Only cancer drugs with a randomised trial that measured survival are counted so far.

The clock

  • It starts the day the FDA received the application and stops the day the drug was approved. Both dates are the FDA's own.
  • It never pauses. Where the FDA rejected an application before approving it later, that time counts, and the drug is labelled "Rejected first". The FDA has not published every rejection letter, so some long waits carry no label.
  • Days beyond the benchmark are the days at the FDA minus the benchmark.

The benchmark

  • 60 days: the FDA's national priority voucher programme targets a review of one to two months after final submission, against the usual 10 to 12. Its fastest reviews so far took 44 to 61 days, counted from the day it accepted the application rather than the day it arrived.
  • 21 days: the first COVID-19 vaccine was authorised for emergency use 21 days after the request was filed.
  • 30 days: a target, not a record. What a very competent agency would aim for.

What is not here yet

  • The human cost of the other drugs. Most were never tested for how much longer patients live, so their cost is harder to count. It is not zero.
  • Drugs still waiting for a decision today.
  • Vaccines, gene therapies and blood products, which a different FDA centre reviews.
